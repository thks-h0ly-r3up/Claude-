Introduction: Nobody Told Me the Veil Lifting Would Hurt

Everybody wants the part where the curtain lifts and light floods the room. Nobody talks about the part where the room has been dark so long, your eyes burn.

I've lived in the dark and called it home. Called it safe. Called it normal. Then something got ripped open, and I could see. What I saw wasn't pretty. It was the truth, though. And the truth, on day one, feels a whole lot like losing something.

That's what this week is. Seven days on the shattering that comes after the veil lifts: the false safety that breaks, the mask that gets heavy, the mirror that cracks, the grief that shows up late, the weird silence after the chains drop. Stuff you won't see on a pretty quote card.

If you're tired, shaky, mad, numb, or crying in a parking lot, you're not failing at faith. A body that finally stops surviving sometimes falls apart a little. That's a nervous system exhaling, not a verdict on your soul.

I'm not above you. I'm not healed up and tied with a bow. I'm walking beside you with a service dog, a Bible, and a lot of rebuilding left. I write from thanks for what God Almighty has already carried me through, and I write from the places that still ache. Both can sit at the same table.

Grounding invitation (any time): If you want, put one hand on your chest or your dog or the table. Feel something solid. Let your eyes stay open or close them, whichever feels safer. Take one breath that's longer out than in. You can stop any time.

Day 1 — God Tore It, Not You

The shattering of false safety

Sanctuary

Find your feet on the floor. Notice one thing you can see, one you can hear. Open your hands on your lap if that feels okay.

Prayer of thanksgiving: God Almighty, thank You that I'm still here. Thank You for every mile You carried me that I never saw. Thank You for the light, even when it stings. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Matthew 27:50–51; Hebrews 10:19–20 (also see Mark 15:37–38 and Luke 23:45). Paraphrase: at Christ's death the temple curtain split from top to bottom, and Hebrews says the way into God's presence is opened through His flesh.

Look at where the tear starts. Top to bottom. Not bottom up. That means no person ripped it. No amount of my self-help hustle ripped it. God did.

Here's the part nobody says: for a lot of us, the curtain wasn't just between us and God. It was a whole system of "safe." Rules that kept us small. People we obeyed so we wouldn't get hurt. Silence we kept so the peace would hold. That was a veil, and it was holding us up in a weird way. When it tears, the "safe" tears with it.

So if you feel gutted, not glad, after you finally saw the truth, you're not ungrateful. You lost the shelter you built to survive. That's real. God can tear a curtain and still sit with you in the rubble.

Practical difference: Spiritual language says "He tore it." Behavioral recovery says "I still have to choose a new safe place today." Both are true. Don't skip the second.

Contextual Insight

History (not canon): The first-century Jewish historian Josephus describes the temple's inner curtain as a huge, richly woven Babylonian tapestry (Jewish War 5.5.4, §§212–214; check the Whiston or Loeb edition and note the translator). Look up how thick and large later sources claim it was, and compare them.

Church tradition: Ethiopian Orthodox churches keep the Maqdas (the holy of holies) behind curtains, where the tabot rests. Ask your priest how the curtain is understood in your parish. I'm not speaking for the Church on that; I'm inviting you to ask.

Sacred Reflection

1. What did my "safe" cost me?

2. What did I lose when I saw clearly that nobody's naming?

3. Where could I let God sit in the rubble with me, no fixing?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Write "what I lost" on a sticky note. Just the list, no explanation. Put it in your Bible, a jar, or a pocket. Low-energy option: say one lost thing out loud to God, or to Big Boy-level honesty in the car.

Supplication

Lord, the curtain's down and I'm standing in the open. I'm scared. I miss the thing that kept me small, and I'm mad I miss it. You tore it, so I know You're in the room. Sit here with me. Show me one safe place today. Not forever. Today. I'm tired, and You're not. Amen.

Declarations

  • The tear came from God's hand, not my failure.
  • I can grieve the shelter and still choose the light.
  • I am not alone in the open.

Verify It Yourself

Don't take my word for it. Open the source and check the pattern yourself. Read Matthew 27:51 and Mark 15:38 side by side. Search the exact phrase "from the top to the bottom" in your Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo text. Write down the translator and edition. Check the surrounding verses. Then bring one question about the curtain to your priest or a trusted teacher. Both sides: some scholars read the tearing as judgment on the temple; others as access to God. Read one of each and see which sits with the text.

Day 2 — The Mask That Fades

The weight of performing "I'm fine"

Sanctuary

Drop your shoulders an inch. Unclench your jaw. If your eyes want to close, let them. If not, find one color in the room and rest on it.

Prayer of thanksgiving: Father, thank You for every day I got through while pretending. Thank You that You knew the real me the whole time. Thank You for strength I didn't know I had. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Exodus 34:29–35; 2 Corinthians 3:12–13. Paraphrase: Moses' face shone after speaking with God, he put a covering over it when speaking to the people, and Paul says he did so partly so they would not stare at the fading.

Everybody preaches the shining. Nobody preaches the fading. Paul says the glory on Moses' face was passing away, and the veil kept folks from watching it go.

Hold up. Think about who else wears a veil because they don't want anyone to see the glow dim. Strong women. The one who holds everybody together. The survivor everyone calls "so brave" while she's coming apart in the shower. We say "I'm good" and smile, because if we stopped, somebody might see how tired we are.

The mask isn't a sin. It kept you functioning. But it's heavy, and God never asked you to carry it alone. When the veil lifts, the first thing people see is not a shining saint. It's a person. And Christ meets persons.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "unveiled face." Behavioral recovery: tell one safe person one true thing. Not everyone. One.

Contextual Insight

Translation note: The Hebrew verb in Exodus 34 (qaran) is rendered "shone" in most English versions. Jerome's Latin Vulgate has cornuta ("horned"), which is why some medieval art shows Moses with horns. Look up the word in a Hebrew lexicon and see how the Septuagint (Greek) handles it. The Ethiopian tradition's Old Testament follows the Septuagint lineage, so ask your priest or a Ge'ez scholar how the Ge'ez reads it. I haven't verified the Ge'ez wording myself.

Patristic: John Chrysostom preached on 2 Corinthians 3 in his Homilies on 2 Corinthians. Find an edition (note the translator, e.g. in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series) and read his section on the veil before you trust any summary, including mine.

Sacred Reflection

1. Who do I perform "fine" for?

2. What would they see if the mask slipped?

3. What would it feel like to be tired in front of one safe person?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Text or tell one safe person: "Honestly, today I'm ___." One word. If nobody feels safe, whisper it to God, or write it on paper and tear the paper up. Low-energy option: pick a feeling emoji and send it with no words.

Supplication

Lord, I'm tired of holding this face up. You saw the glow and You see the fade. Let me be a person in front of You. Give me one safe human. Help me know the difference between privacy and hiding, and forgive me when I can't tell yet. Amen.

Declarations

  • I don't have to shine to be loved.
  • My tiredness is not a failure of faith.
  • I get to choose who sees me, and I get to choose someone.

Verify It Yourself

Read Exodus 34:29–35 and 2 Corinthians 3:7–18 in full. Check the surrounding context, not just the pull-quote. Which verse says the glory was fading, and which says it was veiled? Write down the translator and edition. Both sides: some read Paul as saying Moses was hiding the end of the old covenant; others as Paul's allegory about hearts. Read one commentary from each lens, and ask a priest which the Church's commentary tradition (the Andemta) leans on.

Day 3 — The Mirror Cracks

Seeing clearly, with mercy

Sanctuary

Press your palms together, then let them go. Feel the warmth. Name three things you can touch right now.

Prayer of thanksgiving: God Almighty, thank You for the truth, even when it shows up loud. Thank You for every time You kept me alive when I didn't know how. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: 2 Corinthians 3:18; James 1:23–25. Paraphrase: believers with unveiled faces look at the Lord's glory and are changed; a person who hears but doesn't act is like someone who glances at their face in a mirror and forgets what they saw.

Here's one nobody wants to say out loud. When the veil lifts, you don't just see who hurt you. You see the whole story. The people you thought were safe. The version of yourself you made up so you could stand it. The moves you made to survive that you aren't proud of.

Hear me clear: survival moves aren't sins you owe shame for. Silence, lying to keep peace, staying too long, leaving too fast. That was your nervous system doing its best with what it had. Seeing it isn't a trial. It's a mirror, and mercy goes in front of the mirror.

Also hear this: seeing clearly doesn't mean you must confront everybody this week. Truth can be held privately for a while. Wisdom, safety, and timing are not cowardice.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "beholding." Behavioral recovery: make a safety plan before any hard conversation. A counselor or advocate can help with that.

Contextual Insight

Historical note: Mirrors in Paul's Corinth were polished bronze, not glass; Corinth was known for them. The image was real but dim. Paul uses that in 1 Corinthians 13:12 too (check how your translation renders it). Look up "Corinthian bronze mirrors" in a reliable commentary and see how the dimness shapes the meaning.

Scholarly theory: Some scholars read "beholding" in 2 Cor 3:18 as "reflecting." The Greek verb (katoptrizomai) can mean either. Compare two translations and write down which you used.

Sacred Reflection

1. What do I see now that I couldn't see before?

2. Which survival move can I thank instead of shame?

3. What truth can I hold quietly until I'm safe to say it?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Write "Thank you for keeping me alive" to one survival move (example: "Thank you, silence"). Three sentences max. Low-energy option: say "thank you, I did my best" while looking at your own hands.

Supplication

Lord, the mirror cracked and I saw everything. Some of it I'm furious about. Some of it I'm ashamed of. Cover the shame with mercy, and give me wisdom about what to do with the truth. Don't let me rush. Don't let me hide forever. Teach me the pace. Amen.

Declarations

  • What I did to survive, I did with what I had.
  • I can see clearly and still be gentle with myself.
  • My timing for truth is mine to pray over and plan.

Verify It Yourself

Read James 1:22–25 and 2 Corinthians 3:17–18 in full. Search the exact phrase "as in a mirror" in your Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo source and in one Western translation. Note any difference between "beholding" and "reflecting." Write down the translator and edition. Both sides: check how a source outside our tradition reads the verse and what it says about shame. Then ask a priest or counselor how repentance differs from shame. They are not the same thing.

Day 4 — The Grief Shows Up Late

Mourning after you're finally safe

Sanctuary

Slow one breath out, like fogging a window. If tears are close, let them be close. If they're not, that's okay too.

Prayer of thanksgiving: Father, thank You for the day I could finally breathe. Thank You for every tear You've counted. Thank You for staying while I fell apart. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Isaiah 25:6–8. Paraphrase: on the mountain, God removes the covering cast over all peoples, swallows up death, and wipes tears from every face.

Nobody warned me about this. While I was in survival mode, I couldn't cry. There was no time. The second things got safe, the grief showed up. Years of it. Wrong time, wrong place, in the grocery store.

That's not a setback. That's your body finally saying, "Now I can feel it." Isaiah says God removes the covering (some translations say shroud) that's over the people. A covering is thick. It numbs. When it lifts, what was under it comes up.

You are allowed to grieve what happened, what you lost, and who you could have been without it. Grief and gratitude can both be on the table. You don't have to pick.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "God wipes the tears." Behavioral recovery: sleep, food, water, a counselor, a friend. Tears need a body that's cared for.

Contextual Insight

Translation note: The Hebrew word often rendered "covering" or "shroud" in Isaiah 25:7 is uncommon. Look it up in a Hebrew lexicon and compare three translations. Write down which words they choose. The Ge'ez reading is for you or your priest to check; I haven't verified it myself.

Church tradition: Ethiopian Orthodox funeral and memorial rites include lament and prayers for the departed. Ask your priest how your parish holds mourning, and whether there's a practice for the griefs that have no funeral (lost childhood, lost trust).

Sacred Reflection

1. What grief showed up after I got safe?

2. What loss never got a funeral?

3. What would it mean to let that grief sit at the table without rushing it?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Light a candle (or a flashlight). Say one loss out loud. Blow it out (or click it off). That's the funeral for today. Low-energy option: drink a full glass of water slowly and say, "That mattered."

Supplication

God, the grief is here and it's big. I didn't expect it now. Hold me up while it moves. Don't let me drown in it, and don't let me run from it. Wipe the tears You promised to wipe, and send a human to sit with me. Amen.

Declarations

  • My grief is proof I'm safe enough to feel.
  • I can mourn and still be grateful.
  • God counts every tear and does not rush me.

Verify It Yourself

Read Isaiah 25:6–9 in full, not just verse 8. Search the exact phrase "wipe away tears" in your Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo source and compare it with Revelation 21:4. Check the translator and edition. Both sides: some commentators read Isaiah 25 as pointing to a future feast; others as an image of present restoration too. Read one of each. If grief feels bigger than you can carry, a licensed counselor is a wise partner. That is care, not weak faith.

Day 5 — Seen in the Wilderness

Hagar and the God who sees

Sanctuary

Look at something small in the room, a crack in the wall, a mug, a leaf, for a full breath. You are seen too.

Prayer of thanksgiving: God Almighty, thank You for seeing me when nobody else did. Thank You for every wilderness You walked into after me. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Genesis 16:7–14. Paraphrase: Hagar, mistreated and running, is found by the angel of the Lord at a spring in the wilderness, and she names God "the one who sees me."

Hagar is the one nobody preaches right. An enslaved woman, used, pushed out, pregnant, alone. She's the only person in Scripture who gives God a name, and the name is about being seen.

When the veil lifts, one of the cruelest parts is realizing how long nobody saw. You were hurting in plain sight. Hagar got a God who walked into the desert, asked her name, and asked, "Where are you going?" Not as a trap. As a way to say, "I see you and I'm not leaving."

Say it plain: being unseen was harm. Being seen by God is not a cure, but it is a floor. And you can stand on a floor.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "the God who sees." Behavioral recovery: find one person or place where your story is believed. A support group, advocate, or counselor can help.

Contextual Insight

Text note: The name in Genesis 16:13 is often transliterated El Roi. Look up the grammar: it can mean "God of seeing" or "God who sees me." Compare translations, and note how the verse ends (the well's name). Verify the Ge'ez reading with a scholar or priest.

Church tradition: Hagar appears in Orthodox reading as a figure of the outcast whom God does not abandon. Ask your priest how she is treated in your tradition's commentaries, and whether she is read differently elsewhere. Check both readings; Paul uses her allegorically in Galatians 4, which many modern readers find uncomfortable. Read it in context before you decide.

Sacred Reflection

1. When did I feel unseen, even in a crowd?

2. What does it change to say, "God saw that"?

3. Who has seen me clearly and stayed?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Name your own "El Roi" moment: write one sentence about a time someone, or God, noticed you at your lowest. Thank them for it, in your head or on paper. Low-energy option: look in the mirror for ten seconds and say, "I see you."

Supplication

Lord, You are the one who sees me. You saw when no one else did. Tell me where I'm running from and where You're leading. Help me believe my story was real. Put a human in my path who will not look away. Amen.

Declarations

  • What happened to me was real, and it was seen.
  • I am not invisible to God Almighty.
  • I can stand on being seen.

Verify It Yourself

Read Genesis 16 and Genesis 21:8–21 together, so you have both of Hagar's wilderness scenes. Search "the God who sees" and check the translator's note on the Hebrew name. Note the edition. Both sides: read Galatians 4:21–31 and then read one commentary that defends Paul's use and one that questions it. Decide with the text open, not with my opinion.

Day 6 — The Loud Quiet After the Chains

Freedom that feels like loss

Sanctuary

Wiggle your fingers. Roll your neck slowly. Notice the quiet in the room, whatever it sounds like.

Prayer of thanksgiving: Father, thank You that the chains are broken. Thank You for every door You opened, even the ones I walked through shaking. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Psalm 107:10–16 (note: verse numbering can differ between Psalter traditions; use the numbering in your own source). Paraphrase: those who sat in darkness and gloom, bound in affliction, cried to the Lord, and He brought them out of the dark and broke their chains and bars of bronze.

Nobody tells you the sound of freedom. It's quiet. Weirdly quiet. No yelling, no walking on eggshells, no emergency. And your body doesn't know what to do with it.

So you pick fights. You scroll at 3 a.m. You miss people who weren't good for you. You feel guilty for being okay. You think, "Is something wrong?" Nothing's wrong. Your nervous system is learning that calm isn't a trap.

The Psalm says God broke the chains. It doesn't say they stopped feeling like weight the next morning. A body that wore chains for years still moves like it's chained. That's not rebellion against freedom. It's recovery.

Also: when you change, some relationships shift. People who liked you small may not like you whole. That hurts, and it's real. You can grieve that and still keep walking.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "chains broken." Behavioral recovery: routines, sleep, boundaries, safe people, and a professional if flashbacks or panic spike.

Contextual Insight

Text note: The Psalter numbering in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo tradition follows the Septuagint lineage, which numbers some psalms differently from most English Bibles. Check which number your source uses for this psalm before you cite it. That's exactly the kind of thing worth confirming rather than assuming.

History: "Bars of bronze" in the ancient world were gate bars on city walls and prison doors. Look up how ancient city gates and prisons were built to see the image the psalmist was using.

Sacred Reflection

1. What does calm feel like in my body: a gift, or a threat?

2. Which relationship changed since I got free, and what do I feel about it?

3. What is one tiny, steady thing I can add that says, "This is safe now"?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Do one calm thing on purpose: make tea, fold three towels, or step outside for two minutes. Tell your body, "This is quiet, and quiet is allowed." Low-energy option: just sit with the light off and say, "I'm safe enough right now."

Supplication

Lord, You broke the chains, and I'm learning to walk. The quiet feels strange. Teach my body that peace isn't a trap. Be near the people I miss, and give me strength to keep the boundaries I built. Don't let me go back because the silence is loud. Amen.

Declarations

  • Quiet is not danger.
  • I can miss what hurt me and still choose what heals me.
  • The chains are broken. I am learning to move.

Verify It Yourself

Read Psalm 107 in full and note the repeated phrase after each rescue ("let them give thanks…"). Search that phrase in your Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo source and check the psalm number. Write down the edition and translator. Both sides: read one trauma-informed counselor's perspective on why calm can feel unsafe after long stress, and one pastoral source on it. Compare. If panic, flashbacks, or urges to hurt yourself spike, reach out to a licensed professional or call or text 988.

Day 6 Coloring: The Broken Chain and the Cross

Use the leather brown for the cross. Make the sky as loud as the quiet isn't.

Day 7 — Rebuild With Open Hands

Nehemiah on the broken wall

Sanctuary

Open your hands, palms up, on your lap. Feel what it's like to hold nothing and still be held.

Prayer of thanksgiving: God Almighty, thank You for the week. Thank You for every piece You handed back to me. Thank You for a rebuild that starts with one brick. Amen.

Scripture & Teaching

Read: Nehemiah 2:11–18. Paraphrase: Nehemiah inspects Jerusalem's broken walls by night, tells the people the hand of God has been good to him, and they say, "Let us rise up and build," and strengthen their hands for the good work.

Look at what Nehemiah did first. He didn't start building. He walked the ruins at night and looked at the damage. Quietly. Then he told the truth out loud. Then they built, a stretch each, each family working on the wall by their own house.

That's the shape of rebuilding. Look honest. Say it plain. Build one section. Not the whole wall this week.

The veil is gone, and you see the rubble. Rubble isn't the end of the story; it's the building site. You don't have to build alone, and you don't have to build it all. Pick your section.

And this is where I say what I always say: we survived. Now we rebuild. Rebuilding still means rest, boundaries, help, and doing it slow. Gratitude for what's been carried, honesty about what hurts, one next brick.

Practical difference: Spiritual language: "the good hand of God." Behavioral recovery: a plan with small steps, a support person, and a date.

Contextual Insight

History: Nehemiah is set in the Persian period, after the exile. Some Ethiopian and Septuagint-lineage books number Ezra–Nehemiah differently (for example, "Ezra Sutuel"/2 Esdras traditions); check how your Ethiopian source labels the book before you cite it. Archaeologists have also uncovered wall sections in Jerusalem; look up recent summaries from a university or museum source and note which dates are debated.

Church tradition: Ask your priest how the Church reads Nehemiah's work spiritually (rebuilding, repentance, community). That's their lane; I'm only pointing you to the question.

Sacred Reflection

1. What is my "section of the wall"?

2. Who builds beside me, or who could?

3. What is one brick I can lay this week?

Soft Action (10 minutes or less)

Write your one brick on a card. Set a reminder for it. Tell one person, or just tell God. Low-energy option: choose your brick and sleep on it. Choosing counts.

Supplication

Lord, I see the rubble and I see You in it. Strengthen my hands for the work You've given me, one stretch at a time. Send builders beside me. Keep me from rushing and from quitting. When I'm worn out, remind me that resting is part of building. Amen.

Declarations

  • I can look at the damage and not be destroyed by it.
  • I build my section, not the whole wall.
  • God Almighty's hand has been good to me, and I am still here.

Verify It Yourself

Read Nehemiah 1–3 and note how the chapters name who built which section. Search the exact phrase "let us rise up and build" in your Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo source and one other translation. Check the book title and numbering used. Write down the edition. Both sides: compare a devotional reading of Nehemiah (rebuild your life) with a historical-critical reading (rebuild a city). Which parts apply to us, and which don't?

Final Reflection

You made it through seven days of the part nobody puts on the poster. Look back across the week.

1. Which day hit the hardest, and what did it show me?

2. What am I grateful God Almighty carried me through?

3. What is the one brick I'm laying next?

Final step (10 minutes or less): Pick your favorite colored page. Put it where you'll see it. If you can, send one line to one person: "I finished something hard." Low-energy: just put the page where you'll see it.

Closing Prayer

God Almighty, thank You for this week. Thank You for every veil You lifted and every one You're still lifting. Thank You for holding me in the open. Keep my eyes honest and my heart soft. Send safe people. Teach me rest. When I'm weak, remember me. We survived. Now let's go back, with truth and a hand out. Souls are attached to my obedience. You get the glory. Amen.
